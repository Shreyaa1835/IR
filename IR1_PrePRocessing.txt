# Practical: Text Preprocessing using NLTK

## 1. AIM

To perform text preprocessing using the NLTK library by applying the following techniques:

1. Convert text into lowercase.
2. Remove special characters and numbers.
3. Tokenize the text into individual words.
4. Remove stop words.
5. Perform stemming to convert words into their root form.

---

## 2. Concepts Used in This Practical

### 1. NLP (Natural Language Processing)
NLP is a branch of Artificial Intelligence that enables computers to understand, process and analyze human language.

**Example:** Chatbots, language translation, sentiment analysis and text summarization.

### 2. NLTK
NLTK stands for Natural Language Toolkit.

It is a Python library used for processing human language and performing NLP tasks.

**Uses:**
- Tokenization
- Stop word removal
- Stemming
- Lemmatization
- Text analysis

### 3. Text Preprocessing
Text preprocessing is the process of cleaning and preparing raw text before providing it to an NLP or Machine Learning model.

**Example:**  
Original: `"The Students are Learning NLP!"`  
Preprocessed: `"student learn nlp"`

### 4. Tokenization
Tokenization is the process of dividing a sentence or paragraph into smaller units called tokens.

**Example:**

Input:
`"I love Python programming"`

Output:
`['I', 'love', 'Python', 'programming']`

### 5. Stop Word Removal
Stop words are commonly used words that generally carry less importance for many NLP tasks.

Examples: `is`, `the`, `a`, `an`, `and`, `from`, `to`.

Removing these words helps reduce unnecessary text.

### 6. Stemming
Stemming is the process of reducing words to their root or stem form by removing prefixes or suffixes.

Examples:

| Original Word | Stemmed Word |
|---|---|
| playing | play |
| studies | studi |
| processes | process |
| finding | find |
| unnecessary | unnecessari |

**Important:** Stemming does not always produce a meaningful English dictionary word.

# 3. Line-by-Line Code Explanation

### Step 1: Importing Libraries

**Line 1:**
`import nltk`

- Imports the NLTK library.
- NLTK is used for Natural Language Processing.
- It provides tools for tokenization, stemming and stop word removal.

**External question: What is NLTK?**

Answer: NLTK stands for Natural Language Toolkit. It is a Python library used for processing and analyzing human language.

**Line 2:**
`import re`

- Imports Python's regular expression module.
- It is used to search, match and replace patterns in text.
- Here, it helps remove special characters and numbers.

**External question: What is Regular Expression?**

Answer: A regular expression is a pattern used to search, match or manipulate text.

**Line 3:**
`from nltk.corpus import stopwords`

- Imports the stopwords dataset from NLTK.
- It contains commonly used words in different languages.
- We will use English stop words in this practical.

**Line 4:**
`from nltk.stem import PorterStemmer`

- Imports PorterStemmer from NLTK.
- It is used to convert words into their stem form.

**External question: What is Porter Stemmer?**

Answer: Porter Stemmer is an algorithm used to reduce English words to their stem by removing common suffixes.

### Step 2: Downloading Stop Words

**Line 5:**
`nltk.download('stopwords')`

- Downloads the stopwords dataset required by NLTK.
- Without downloading it for the first time, the program may show a missing resource error.
- It downloads the English stop words dataset along with other available language lists.

**External question: Why do we download stopwords?**

Answer: We download stopwords because NLTK needs this dataset to identify and remove common words from the text.

### Step 3: Defining Original Text

**Line 6–9:**
`text = """..."""`

- Stores the paragraph in a variable named `text`.
- Triple quotation marks allow us to write text across multiple lines.
- This is the original input given to the preprocessing program.

**External question: Why are triple quotes used?**

Answer: Triple quotes are used to store multi-line strings in Python.

### Step 4: Displaying Original Text

**Line 10–11:**
`print("Original Text:")`  
`print(text)`

- Displays the heading and original paragraph.
- This helps us compare the original text with the preprocessed text.

### Step 5: Converting Text into Lowercase

**Line 12:**
`text = text.lower()`

- Converts all uppercase letters into lowercase.
- This ensures that words with different capitalization are treated as the same word.

**Example:**

`"Information Retrieval"` becomes `"information retrieval"`.

**External question: Why convert text into lowercase?**

Answer: To maintain consistency and avoid treating uppercase and lowercase versions of the same word as different words.

### Step 6: Removing Special Characters and Numbers

**Line 13:**
`text = re.sub(r'[^a-z\s]', '', text)`

This is one of the most important lines.

- `re.sub()` is used to replace matching patterns.
- `r'...'` represents a raw string.
- `[^a-z\s]` identifies characters that are NOT lowercase English letters or whitespace.
- `a-z` represents lowercase letters from a to z.
- `\s` represents whitespace, including spaces and newline characters.
- `''` means replace the matching characters with nothing.

**Example:**

Input: `"Hello! NLP 123"`  
Output: `"hello nlp "`

**External question: What is the use of re.sub()?**

Answer: `re.sub()` replaces matching patterns in a string with another value. Here, it removes special characters and numbers.

### Step 7: Tokenization

**Line 14:**
`words = text.split()`

- Splits the cleaned text into individual words.
- Stores all words in a list called `words`.
- Each individual word becomes a separate token.

**Example:**

Input: `"information retrieval is useful"`

Output:
`['information', 'retrieval', 'is', 'useful']`

**External question: What is the difference between a string and a list?**

Answer: A string stores text as a sequence of characters, whereas a list stores multiple elements as separate items.

**Line 15–16:**
`print("\nTokenized Words:")`  
`print(words)`

- Displays the tokenized words.
- `\n` inserts a new line before the heading.

### Step 8: Loading English Stop Words

**Line 17:**
`stop_words = set(stopwords.words('english'))`

- `stopwords.words('english')` retrieves the English stop words.
- `set()` converts them into a set.
- A set stores unique elements and provides efficient membership checking.

**External question: Why use a set instead of a list?**

Answer: A set provides faster membership checking when determining whether a word is a stop word.

### Step 9: Creating an Empty List

**Line 18:**
`filtered_words = []`

- Creates an empty list.
- This list will store words after removing stop words.

### Step 10: Removing Stop Words

**Line 19:**
`for word in words:`

- Starts a for loop.
- Iterates through every word in the tokenized list.
- Each word is temporarily stored in the variable `word`.

**Line 20:**
`if word not in stop_words:`

- Checks whether the current word is NOT present in the stop words set.
- If the word is not a stop word, the condition becomes true.

**Line 21:**
`filtered_words.append(word)`

- Adds the word to the `filtered_words` list.
- Stop words are skipped and are not added.

**Example:**

Before stop word removal:

`['information', 'retrieval', 'is', 'the', 'process', 'of', 'finding']`

After stop word removal:

`['information', 'retrieval', 'process', 'finding']`

**External question: Why do we remove stop words?**

Answer: Stop word removal reduces unnecessary words and helps focus on meaningful terms for many NLP tasks.

### Step 11: Displaying Filtered Words

**Line 22–23:**
`print("\nAfter Stop Word Removal:")`  
`print(filtered_words)`

- Displays the list after removing stop words.
- This shows the result of the stop word removal process.

### Step 12: Creating a Stemming Object

**Line 24:**
`stemmer = PorterStemmer()`

- Creates an object of the PorterStemmer class.
- This object is used to perform stemming on individual words.

**External question: Which stemming algorithm is used here?**

Answer: Porter Stemming Algorithm.

### Step 13: Creating an Empty List for Stemmed Words

**Line 25:**
`stemmed_words = []`

- Creates an empty list.
- It stores the stemmed versions of the filtered words.

### Step 14: Performing Stemming

**Line 26:**
`for word in filtered_words:`

- Iterates through each word remaining after stop word removal.

**Line 27:**
`stemmed_words.append(stemmer.stem(word))`

- `stemmer.stem(word)` converts the current word into its stem form.
- `append()` adds the stemmed word to the `stemmed_words` list.

**Example:**

| Original Word | Stemmed Word |
|---|---|
| finding | find |
| removes | remov |
| unnecessary | unnecessari |
| words | word |
| reduces | reduc |

**External question: What is the difference between stemming and stop word removal?**

Answer:
- Stop word removal eliminates commonly used words.
- Stemming reduces remaining words to their stem form.

### Step 15: Displaying Stemmed Words

**Line 28–29:**
`print("\nAfter Stemming:")`  
`print(stemmed_words)`

- Displays the final list of stemmed words.
- These words have undergone stop word removal and stemming.

### Step 16: Displaying Final Preprocessed Text

**Line 30:**
`print("\nFinal Preprocessed Text:")`

- Displays the heading for the final output.

**Line 31:**
`print(" ".join(stemmed_words))`

- `" ".join()` combines all elements of the list into a single string.
- A space is inserted between every word.
- Displays the final preprocessed paragraph.

**Example:**

List:
`['inform', 'retriev', 'process', 'find']`

Final string:
`inform retriev process find`

**External question: What is the use of join()?**

Answer: The `join()` method combines elements of a list into a single string using a specified separator.

# 5. Complete AIM-Based Theory

### Q1. What is Text Preprocessing?

Text preprocessing is the process of cleaning and transforming raw text into a structured format suitable for NLP and Machine Learning algorithms.

### Q2. Why is text preprocessing important?

It:
- Removes unnecessary information.
- Reduces text complexity.
- Improves consistency.
- Helps NLP models process text effectively.

### Q3. What are the main steps of text preprocessing?

1. Lowercase conversion
2. Removing special characters
3. Tokenization
4. Stop word removal
5. Stemming

### Q4. What is the difference between Stemming and Lemmatization?

| Stemming | Lemmatization |
|---|---|
| Removes prefixes or suffixes using rules. | Converts words into their dictionary base form. |
| May produce meaningless words. | Usually produces meaningful words. |
| Faster and simpler. | More linguistically informed. |
| Example: studies → studi | Example: studies → study |

### Q5. What is the difference between Tokenization and Stemming?

- Tokenization divides text into individual words.
- Stemming reduces those individual words to their root or stem form.

### Q6. What is a Corpus in NLTK?

A corpus is a collection of written or spoken text used for language processing and analysis.

### Q7. What are the applications of Text Preprocessing?

- Search engines
- Information retrieval
- Sentiment analysis
- Spam detection
- Text classification
- Chatbots
- Document summarization

### Q8. What is Information Retrieval?

Information Retrieval is the process of finding relevant information or documents from a large collection based on a user's query.

**Example:** Searching for a particular topic on Google.

### Q9. What is the difference between a token and a stop word?

A token is an individual unit of text, such as a word. A stop word is a commonly used word that may be removed during preprocessing.

### Q10. Does stemming always produce a meaningful English word?

No. Stemming may produce a non-dictionary word because it removes word endings using predefined rules.

Example: `unnecessary → unnecessari`.
